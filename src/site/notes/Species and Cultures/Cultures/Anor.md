## Overview

Anor culture is built around three gods. Its people worship [[Religion and Deities/Deific Figures/Gods/Mendura, God of Courage\|Mendura]], [[Religion and Deities/Deific Figures/Gods/Arloya, God of Devotion\|Arloya]], and [[Religion and Deities/Deific Figures/Gods/Coptimaine, God of Belief\|Coptimaine]] together as a single faith. They hold the balance among courage, devotion, and hope to be the pattern of a good life. That pattern reaches into the grammar of the Anoran language, the shape of Anor buildings, and the order of an Anor meal.

Anor is the dominant culture of [[Places/Realms/Alora/Nations/The Rayhostian Theocracy\|The Rayhostian Theocracy]]. Its social order is feudal and bound closely to the faith, which shapes schooling, healing, and daily practice alongside worship.

## Origins & Distribution

Anor culture took shape in the time after the Human Uprising of 4287, when the humans of the eastern continent of [[Places/Realms/Alora/Regions/The Crimson Lands\|The Crimson Lands]] threw off dragon rule and founded the Theocracy. Anor tradition holds that Mendura, Arloya, and Coptimaine aided the rebels in their victory. Faith in the three gods became the center of the new nation's life, and over the generations its culture drifted away from the one the rebels had known under dragon rule.

Anor is dominant across most of the Theocracy today. Along the coasts of the strait between Narathatath and the Theocracy, the more common culture is [[Species and Cultures/Cultures/Trow\|Trow]]. It is the culture of the [[Species and Cultures/Races/Trox\|Trox]] who came up from the caverns, shaped over time by both Anor and [[Species and Cultures/Cultures/Drako\|Drako]].

## Social Structure

Anor society follows a feudal hierarchy. Beneath the monarch stand dukes, counts, barons, and knights, with citizens below them and serfs at the base. Each noble must receive a religious blessing to hold position, and the monarch is held to rule by divine mandate.

Movement between ranks is possible. An exceptional demonstration of virtue can raise a person's standing, and the church offers a path of advancement open to anyone regardless of birth. Military service and charitable works are other recognized routes to nobility.

Villages manage their local affairs through Virtue Councils of three elected members, one representing the domain of each god. Each council answers to the noble who holds its land, whether a baron, count, or duke, since the land and authority over it belong to that lord. Cities keep larger councils with the same three-part voting structure and the same obligation to their lord.

Families are nuclear, though three-generation households are favored where possible. Grandparents teach virtue through stories, parents provide guidance and protection, and children owe obedience and help with community service. The elderly are revered as living examples of virtue practiced over a lifetime, and they hold advisory roles in their families and communities.

## Values & Beliefs

The Anor call the harmony among their three virtues the Sacred Balance. Their teaching holds that courage without devotion leads to recklessness, devotion without hope becomes fanaticism, and hope without courage enables evil, so all three must work together.

Anor temples are three-sided, with an altar to each god joined by a central triangular sanctuary. Local temples keep Virtue Keepers, who specialize in one god while honoring all three, and traveling Triad Speakers carry the faith to remote areas. The faithful pray three times daily: at dawn for courage, at noon for devotion, and at sunset for hope.

Anor belief holds that souls pass after death into the Eternal Realm, where they serve the gods according to the virtue that was strongest in their lives. Those who balanced all three become Divine Messengers. The Anor regard magic as a divine gift that demands responsible stewardship.

Anor taboos include acting purely for selfish gain, betraying a sworn oath, refusing aid to those in genuine need, and cowardice in the face of injustice. Nature is considered a gift requiring wise stewardship, and the Anor seek a balance between using resources and preserving them, and waste is held to be sinful.

Outsiders are met with welcome and with caution. A foreigner is judged by their actions and their willingness to respect local customs, and origins count for little. Conversion to the triad faith is welcomed but never forced.

## Daily Life & Customs

### Language and Gesture

Anoran grammar follows the triad. Verbs conjugate in three aspects: Courageous, for acts requiring bravery; Devoted, for acts of loyalty or faith; and Hopeful, for acts that carry hope to others. Nouns take one of three genders named for the gods: Menduric (masculine), Arloyic (feminine), and Coptimaic (neuter).

The written script is triangular, built from three primary stroke directions that each represent a god. Legal documents require the signatures of three witnesses, and sacred texts carry illuminated triangular borders inked in the sacred colors of the three gods.

Oaths are sealed by touching the forehead, the heart, and the hand in turn, and religious ceremonies feature synchronized movements in three parts.

### Life Events

Each newborn receives three blessings within seven days of birth, one from a priest of each god. Tradition holds that the child's first cry reveals which virtue will guide them. At the naming ceremony, the parents choose three names representing the virtues they hope the child will carry.

Anor youth come of age at sixteen through the Triad Trial. It requires a test of physical or moral courage, a demonstration of unwavering devotion, and a significant act of aid to the community. Boys and girls receive the same basic education and virtue training until then, and each chooses a primary virtue to follow on completing the Trial.

Couples marry by exchanging three vows before priests of all three gods. The wedding features three rings, three toasts, and three dances, and noble marriages also receive a blessing of political alliance from the Celestial Court.

The dead are kept through a three-day vigil. Mourners share the brave deeds of the deceased, maintain unbroken prayer, and give to the poor in their honor. Bodies are buried facing the Great Temple, with three items placed beside them, one for each virtue.

### Food

The Anor diet is grain-based and built around communal meals. Traditional dishes include Triad Stew, made with three meats representing the gods, and a braided loaf called Virtue Bread. The evening meal is the most important of the day, when the family gathers.

Food is blessed before eating, and sharing food with travelers or the poor is held to be a sacred duty. Festivals call for Courage Wine, a strong spiced wine; Devotion Honey, pure honey saved for special occasions; and Hope Root, vegetables freely shared with the community. The devout fast one day each week, rotating in honor of each god in turn.

### Dress and Adornment

Common dress is earth-toned and practical, often marked with a small triangle brooch or embroidery. Festival clothing adds bright accents in the colors of the wearer's favored virtue.

Nobles wear garments in the sacred colors of the three gods, with elaborate triangle brooches indicating rank, and their crowns and circlets hold gems representing the three gods. Priests wear robes combining all three gods' colors, and the robes of high-ranking clergy shift color in different light as a sign of divine presence. Common jewelry includes pendants, rings set with three stones, and bracelets bearing virtue symbols.

### Arts and Architecture

Triangular motifs are common in Anor art. Sculpture depicts the three gods in harmony or mortals embodying their virtues. Music is sung in three-part harmony, and the Triad Chant of the temples is arranged for three voices. Popular instruments include the triangular tri-harp, virtue bells in sets of three, and ceremonial horns that need three players.

Epic poems are traditionally told in three parts, following a hero's journey through each virtue. Moral tales feature three characters or three challenges, and sacred texts are written in three columns.

Buildings carry three-peaked roofs, triangular windows, and three-spired towers.

### Festivals and Recreation

The Anor keep the holy days of all three gods. Arloya's Day of Devoted Aid falls on 14 Arlaya in spring, Coptimaine's Day of Aid on 14 Coptiama at summer's end, and Mendura's Day of Shoring on 28 Mendia in late autumn. Winter Reflection, as the old year ends and winter begins, is a time for contemplating the virtuous acts of the months behind.

Popular sports include the tri-joust, a tournament requiring skill with three different weapons, and Virtue Racing, run over obstacle courses that test courage, devotion, and hope. Temple Ball is a team game played on a triangular field. Three-player strategy games, three-sided virtue dice, and riddle contests drawn from religious teaching are common pastimes. Traveling virtue minstrels and passion plays depicting the tales of the gods tour the countryside.

### Schooling, Craft, and Healing

Children attend schools attached to temples, learning scripture alongside mathematics, reading, writing, and practical trades in a curriculum balanced across three pillars: spiritual, intellectual, and practical. Crafts pass through guild apprenticeships, and a master must demonstrate ethical practice as well as skill. Anor craft tradition holds that items made with proper ritual and intent gain minor magical properties.

Anor healing treats body, mind, and spirit together, and physical illness is often read as a spiritual imbalance requiring restoration of virtue alongside medical care. Courage Healers are surgeons and battle medics, Devotion Healers are herbalists and keepers of long-term and mental care, and Hope Healers tend community health, epidemics, and the poor. Temples keep medicinal gardens, and priests trained in each virtue's approach offer counsel to those in emotional distress.

## Cultural Identity

Each Anor life is marked by a primary virtue. Tradition reads it first in a newborn's cry, and the youth chooses it formally on completing the Triad Trial. Festival clothing displays it, and Anor belief holds that it decides how the soul will serve the gods after death. The Sacred Balance asks every Anor to hold all three virtues in harmony, and the ideal of the Divine Messenger honors those who manage it.

## Adaptation & Tensions

Traditional roles assign Mendura's courage to men as warriors, leaders, and protectors. Arloya's devotion falls to women as priests, teachers, and guardians of the family, while anyone may serve Coptimaine's hope. Modern Anor society allows more flexibility in these roles while still honoring the traditional associations.

Several Anor teachings differ from the accounts gathered elsewhere in this archive. The archive records that [[Religion and Deities/Deific Figures/Gods/Wathoria, God of Equity\|Wathoria]] judges each soul and decides which afterlife it enters, and that magic in its present form is attributed to [[Religion and Deities/Deific Figures/Primordials/Murasakino, Purple Primordial Spirit\|Murasakino]]. Anor belief in the Eternal Realm and in magic as a gift of the gods stands apart from both.

## Distinctive Traits

- **Oath gesture**, touching forehead, heart, and hand in turn
- ***Trioveth***, "May the Three watch over you," a common blessing
- **"In three-fold truth"**, the formal promise
- **Prayer at dawn, noon, and sunset**, one for each virtue
- **Food shared with travelers** as a sacred duty

**Noble Houses:** Valcourt, Montrail, Aldermont, Greyholt, Hollowmere, Ashcombe, Brightwater

*Note: Each Anor child receives three given names, one from each virtue, and is known in childhood by the first. On completing the Triad Trial, the youth takes the name of their chosen primary virtue as the name they are known by, keeping the other two for oaths, legal documents, and religious rites. Nobles add their house name (e.g., "Hildegard Constance Clarice of House Valcourt," known as Hildegard after choosing courage). Commoners are known by a parent's name, a trade, or a village. Devout families sometimes give a name derived directly from one of the gods, a choice some clergy consider presumptuous.*
