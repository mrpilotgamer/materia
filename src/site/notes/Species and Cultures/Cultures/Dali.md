## Overview

Dali culture is built around the house, the garden, and the guest. Its people hold that a well-kept home is walled against the world and open to the sky, and that anyone welcomed inside is owed the best the house can give. The Dali prize poetry above every other art, revere light in all its forms, and bargain in the bazaar with a skill few other peoples can match.

## Origins & Distribution

Dali culture is native to the southeastern coast of [[Places/Realms/Alora/Regions/The Jotuun Nexus\|The Jotuun Nexus]], where warm rains and rich soil supported a string of coastal cities. Each grew around its bazaar and its gardens, and each learned early to manage its water with care. When Helano's expansion threatened the city-states of the east, the coastal Dali cities led their unification into [[Places/Realms/Alora/Nations/Khania\|Khania]] in 5117. Khania's alliance with [[Places/Realms/Alora/Regions/Laurland\|Laurland]] kept Helano away from the Dali coast in the years that followed.

Laurland adopted Dali culture slowly over the generations, as its ties to the coast grew closer through trade and alliance. Today Dali is dominant across eastern and southeastern Khania and throughout Laurland. It is also the culture of Khania's government, though the nation as a whole is a mix of Dali and [[Species and Cultures/Cultures/Yotuun\|Yotuun]]. Because Laurland trades with nearly every nation, Dali sailors and merchants are a common sight aboard trading ships across [[Places/Realms/Alora/Alora\|Alora]].

Most Dali are [[Species and Cultures/Races/Grendal\|Grendals]], and most of Khania's Grendals are Dali. [[Species and Cultures/Races/Chisana\|Chisana]] form a sizable minority, and their devotion to the Primordials sits comfortably beside Dali reverence for light.

## Social Structure

The foundation of Dali society is the *khaneh*, the house. A khaneh is an extended household of blood relations, spouses, apprentices, and long-standing dependents, all living around a shared courtyard and eating from a shared cloth. The eldest capable member leads it, and a large khaneh may hold several generations and a trading business under one roof.

The Dali keep no hereditary nobility. A khaneh's standing rests on its *aberu*, a word that means honor or reputation and translates literally as "the water of the face." A house gains aberu through generous hospitality, fair dealing, a well-kept garden, and the learning or verse of its members. It loses aberu through meanness, public disgrace, or unpaid debts. The oldest merchant houses, the most celebrated poets, and the keepers of the great gardens stand highest in practice. The Dali keep no bonded labor, and a house that cannot pay its debts loses face long before it loses property.

Neighborhoods and villages govern their own affairs through the *anjoman*, a council of khaneh heads that meets in a garden. The anjoman settles disputes, oversees the local bazaar, and chooses the *mirab*, the water master who divides the flow of the channels among the houses. The mirab's office is among the most respected a Dali can hold, since a fair division of water keeps a community whole.

## Values & Beliefs

Hospitality is the first duty of every Dali. A guest is fed before any question is asked, given the best seat and the best food, and protected by the house for as long as they stay. Refusing hospitality shames a khaneh, and harming a guest under one's own roof is among the worst acts a Dali can commit.

The Dali hold two standards of honesty and see no contradiction between them. In the bazaar, cleverness is expected, and a trader who is outwitted has only themselves to blame. Under a roof, honesty is owed, and a lie told to a guest or host costs aberu that is slow to return. Outsiders who know the Grendal reputation for deception are often surprised to find a Dali host scrupulously truthful at home and cheerfully cunning at market.

The greatest taboo is the despoiling of a garden. A garden holds generations of a household's labor, its standing among its neighbors, and its share of Ki's light, so destroying one strikes at the house itself. Cutting down another's fruit trees, burning or trampling their beds, or fouling their pool brings shame on the offender's entire khaneh. Even feuding houses spare each other's gardens, and a household that lets its own garden wither through neglect loses aberu as well.

Reverence for light runs through Dali life. The Dali honor [[Religion and Deities/Deific Figures/Primordials/Ki, Yellow Primordial Spirit\|Ki]] as the source of all light, greeting the sunrise and keeping lamps burning through the night as a small echo of the sun's gift. Darkness itself carries no sin, but a house left dark when guests may come is thought careless and inhospitable. The gardens are Ki's in a special sense, walled against the world but always open to the sky.

The Dali regard magic as a craft like verse or tilework, esteemed when it serves the garden, the water, the voyage, or the guest. Mages who can find water or conjure light are welcome in any house. Magic used to deceive under a roof is held to the same shame as any other lie told there.

## Daily Life & Customs

### Language and Courtesy

Dalian speech is ornate, and plain speaking can seem cold to a Dali ear. Its most distinctive custom is *taarof*, the ritual of offer and refusal. A host offers, the guest refuses out of courtesy, and the host presses the offer again until the guest accepts. A shopkeeper may refuse payment at first, and a friend may insist a gift is worthless. Everyone involved understands the ritual, and taking a first offer at its word is a mark of poor upbringing.

Verse runs through ordinary conversation. Educated Dali quote lines of poetry to make a point, and answering a quoted line with another is a common game at meals and in the bazaar.

### Gardens and Water

Every Dali house that can afford one keeps a walled garden at its heart, laid out around a central pool with channels dividing the beds. Fruit trees give shade, and flowers are chosen for scent as much as color. The larger gardens belong to wealthy houses or to the community, and their keepers are respected craftsmen.

Water reaches the gardens through *qanats*, underground channels that carry water from distant sources without losing it to the sun. Building and maintaining the qanats is skilled, dangerous work, and the families who do it pass the knowledge down through generations.

### The Bazaar

Each Dali town is built around its bazaar, a network of covered streets where each trade keeps its own lane. Bargaining is expected on nearly everything, and a good haggle is enjoyed by both sides as a contest of wit. Deals are sealed with tea and a handshake, and merchants of standing are known for extending credit on the strength of a buyer's aberu alone.

### Life at Sea

Seafaring customs are strongest in Laurland, though Dali sailors from both branches share them. A ship is treated as a khaneh, and its master, the *nakhoda*, holds the authority of a house head. The crew eat from one cloth, and a guest taken aboard is owed the same hospitality as a guest ashore.

Every Dali ship carries a lamp that is never allowed to go out while the ship is at sea, and a lamp gone dark is taken as a grave omen. Sailors recite verse on the night watches and navigate by the stars, which Dali poets call the lamps of the sky. Pearl diving is a Laurland tradition, and diving families hold their knowledge of the beds as closely as any merchant guards a trade route.

### Life Events

A lamp is lit at the birth of each child and kept burning through the first night. At a naming feast some days later, the family's eldest opens a book of verse at random and reads the first lines aloud as a blessing.

Dali youth come of age at sixteen. Each composes a short verse of their own and recites it before the household and its guests, after which they eat as adults at the family cloth. A poor verse brings teasing and no disgrace, though a fine one is remembered for years.

Couples marry before a wedding cloth laid with a mirror, a pair of lamps, bread, honey, and fruit. The mirror and lamps carry the light into the new household, and the couple see each other first in the mirror's reflection.

The dead are buried within a day. The household keeps a lamp lit in the dead person's memory, and on the fortieth day after the death it gathers family and friends for a meal and a recitation of the verses the departed loved best.

### Food

Meals are eaten seated on carpets around the *sofreh*, a cloth spread on the floor and covered with shared dishes. Rice is the heart of the Dali table, cooked until the grains separate and often crowned with a golden crust from the bottom of the pot. Slow stews of meat, herbs, and fruit accompany it, along with flatbreads, yogurt, and plates of fresh herbs. Pomegranates, saffron, and rosewater are prized, and in Laurland the table leans toward fish and the island's spices. Tea is served at every visit, and a guest is offered sweets before any business is discussed.

### Dress and Adornment

Dali clothing is loose and layered against the sun: long tunics, wide trousers, sashes, and head coverings of light cotton or linen. Embroidery in garden motifs marks festival clothes, and the wealthy wear silk. Laurland Dali favor shorter, practical garments suited to ships and docks, and pearls are the island's signature ornament.

### Arts and Architecture

Poetry is the highest Dali art, and a celebrated poet brings aberu to every house that shelters them. Collections of verse are copied by hand in fine calligraphy, and a family's books are among its most treasured possessions. Carpets are woven with gardens in their patterns, so that a house without room for a garden can still sit in one.

Dali houses face inward. Blank walls meet the street, and the rooms open onto a courtyard garden. Tall wind-catchers draw breezes down into the rooms, and tiles in blue, turquoise, and yellow cover doorways and fountains. Niches for lamps are set into the walls of every room.

### Festivals

The most beloved Dali festival is Yalda, the longest night of the year. Families gather by lamplight and stay awake together until dawn, eating fruit, telling stories, and reading verse aloud. At sunrise they go out into the garden to greet the returning sun, honoring Ki for bringing the light back.

## Cultural Identity

A Dali is known first by their house. The name of one's khaneh, the state of its garden, and the aberu it carries shape how every other Dali receives them. A guest measures a household by its welcome, and a household measures itself by the verse its members can recite and the light it keeps burning.

## Adaptation & Tensions

Dali and Yotuun live side by side in Khania without real hostility, but their customs clash often enough to cause misunderstandings. A Yotuun takes a refusal at its word and so walks away from offers a Dali host meant in earnest. A Yotuun's blunt acceptance of a first offer, meanwhile, strikes the Dali as greedy.

The Yotuun bind themselves with plain oaths before witnesses, while the Dali trust aberu and ornate courtesy, and each finds the other's way of making promises a little hard to read. In Srauga, where the two cultures meet, many families have learned both habits and switch between them as the company requires.

The homeland and Laurland remain one culture with different emphases. Khanian Dali keep more of the life of the house, its gardens, and its hospitality, while Laurland Dali lean toward the sea, the ship, and the trading voyage. Neither branch considers itself the truer one, though each teases the other for its habits.

## Distinctive Traits

- ***Taarof***, the ritual of offer, refusal, and insistence
- **A lamp in a wall niche** by the door, lit for guests after dark
- **Lines of verse quoted** in everyday speech, often answered with another
- **Opening a book of verse at random** for guidance or blessing
- **Tea and sweets** offered before any business is discussed
