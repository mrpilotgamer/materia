*"Lord of Patience" • "Master of Close-Combat"*

## Overview

Selcontri (Sell-CON-tree), Lord of Patience, has guided many down a path that requires no more of them than what is necessary. Doctrine holds that acting for the sake of appearance or excessive pleasure is unnecessary and wholly distasteful. A worshiper of Selcontri understands every action to be deliberate and treats excess in any form as a failure of control. The most efficient path is often the most difficult to walk, since it demands constant vigilance against one's own impulses.

Those who hold themselves back under Selcontri do so from neither pride nor pleasure. They believe it the best path available to them. The god makes clear that restraint is not equivalent to mercy or weakness: if the path one walks requires wrath to be brought down upon foes, then that is the path to walk. Such wrath must be deliberate, calculated, and necessary, and never excessive, wasteful, or performed for show. One who follows the God of Restraint takes a difficult road. For many it brings a peace of mind, and it spares them the unnecessary actions that plague those who cannot control themselves.

### Worshipers

Selcontri's followers include soldiers who value efficient tactics over flashy maneuvers, recovering addicts fighting to control destructive impulses, and monks who have dedicated their lives to mastering themselves. The clergy teaches that true strength lies not in what you can do but in what you choose not to do. Every unnecessary action wastes energy that could be directed toward necessary ends.

The faith differs from those that encourage followers to reach for greatness or indulge their passions. Selcontrian teaching holds to a philosophy of sufficiency. Their temples are austere spaces free of ornamentation, containing what is required for worship and nothing more. The clergy lives by strict codes that govern every aspect of behavior. Their own conduct is offered as evidence that restraint becomes easier with practice, and that unnecessary desires fade when consistently denied.

Clerics of Selcontri work as counselors to those struggling with self-control, trainers in efficient combat technique, and advisors on tactical warfare that minimizes waste of resources and lives. They teach breathing exercises for managing impulses, mental frameworks for distinguishing necessary actions from unnecessary ones, and the principle that temporary discomfort from restraint is preferable to lasting consequences from excess. The monkey is the faith's sacred animal. The creature is capable of great energy and mischief, and once disciplined it shows patience, problem-solving, and the ability to wait for the right moment to act.

### Tenets of Faith

Those who follow Selcontri swear to live by these principles:

1. **My actions are deliberate.** I do no more than required, for doing more is an act of excess.

2. **In battle my mind is clear.** My battle plans are efficient and to the point.

3. **My god is my pillar.** For they are patient and mindful. They are the exemplary example of restraint.

4. **To restrain myself is not equivalent to being merciful.** If the path I walk requires wrath to be brought down upon foes, then that is the path I walk.

## History

Worship predates the [[History/Great Schism War\|Great Schism War]], and the god's philosophy proved particularly valuable during times of scarcity when restraint meant survival. The [[History/Ages/Age of Expansion\|Age of Expansion]] brought Selcontrian teaching into sharp conflict with the dominant ethos of unlimited growth and acquisition, and the clergy warned that empires consuming resources without restraint would face inevitable collapse. Many dismissed these warnings as pessimism. Selcontri's followers practiced careful resource management and avoided the excesses that would later prove catastrophic.

The [[History/Ages/Age of Incursion\|Age of Incursion]] vindicated the philosophy when the [[Religion and Deities/Deific Figures/Primordials\|Primordials]] punished civilizations for their lack of restraint. Communities that had followed Selcontrian principles, taking only what was needed, avoiding waste, and exercising self-control, weathered the disasters better than those that had indulged every impulse.

Worship has grown in the current [[History/Ages/Age of Stability\|Age of Stability]] as societies recognize that maintaining stability requires restraint from excess, and many military commanders have adopted Selcontrian tactical principles that emphasize efficiency over spectacle. The faith stands in philosophical opposition to [[Religion and Deities/Deific Figures/Gods/Alfugel, God of Hatred\|Alfugel, God of Hatred]], whose followers embrace unleashed emotion. The clergy of the two gods clash often in theological debate over whether control or expression serves humanity better.

## Related Entities

**Allies:** [[Religion and Deities/Deific Figures/Gods/Dilliminner, God of Caution\|Dilliminner, God of Caution]] shares Selcontri's philosophy of measured action and avoidance of excess, and both faiths hold that careful preparation and restraint work together to prevent waste and disaster. [[Religion and Deities/Deific Figures/Gods/Arlaxena, God of Leisure\|Arlaxena, God of Leisure]] aligns with doing only what is necessary and avoiding unnecessary effort, though Arlaxena applies this specifically to rest while Selcontri applies it to all actions.

**Rivals:** [[Religion and Deities/Deific Figures/Gods/Alfugel, God of Hatred\|Alfugel, God of Hatred]] teaches that anger should be unleashed instead of restrained, standing in direct philosophical opposition to Selcontri's emphasis on control. [[Religion and Deities/Deific Figures/Gods/Yearma, God of Desire\|Yearma, God of Desire]] encourages indulging desires and pursuing what you crave, which conflicts fundamentally with restraint and self-denial. Many of Yearma's followers are former Selcontrians who found suppressing desires unsustainable. [[Religion and Deities/Deific Figures/Gods/Malbing, God of Consumption\|Malbing, God of Consumption]] honors taking and consuming freely, opposing Selcontri's teaching to consume only what is necessary and no more.

## Holy Days and Festivals

### Day of True Control

Held at the winter solstice, when the year's longest night demands endurance and patience, the Day of True Control is spent doing no more than one must, and nothing beyond what is required by those around them. Followers abstain from all unnecessary actions, pleasures, and indulgences, attending only to essential duties and obligations. The day works as both practice and demonstration of restraint's principles.

Participants rise only when necessary, eat only what suffices, speak only when required, and perform only tasks that cannot be postponed. Selcontrian clergy guide followers in identifying what is truly necessary against what merely feels necessary, and teach the difference between obligation and impulse. New followers find the day difficult and discover how much of their daily activity serves no essential purpose. Veterans of the practice report a peace that comes from acting with complete deliberation.

Some communities hold "competitions of stillness" where participants attempt to remain motionless and silent for extended periods, demonstrating mastery over the impulse to fidget, speak, or move unnecessarily. The monkey inspires the day's observance through its ability to sit still while observing, resisting the urge to swing through trees or chatter. The day closes with a simple acknowledgment that one has successfully exercised control, since celebration would itself be an unnecessary excess.
