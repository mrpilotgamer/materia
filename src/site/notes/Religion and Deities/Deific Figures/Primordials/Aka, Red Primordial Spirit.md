*"Bringer of Destruction" • "Lord of New Life"*

## Overview

Aka (ah-kah), Bringer of Destruction, embodies a truth many mortals struggle to accept. Creation requires destruction. New life springs forth only where old growth has been cleared away, and nature's most violent acts serve renewal. Aka is the volcano that buries fertile valleys in ash and enriches the soil for generations, the wildfire that razes ancient forests and triggers seeds that germinate in nothing but extreme heat, the hurricane that destroys coastal settlements and redistributes nutrients across ecosystems. As one of the fundamental forces that shaped [[Places/Materia\|Materia]], Aka represents destruction as necessity and never as malice: the violent clearing that enables transformation, the catastrophic reset that prevents stagnation.

Volcanic eruptions, earthquakes, tsunamis, and wildfires all fall within Aka's domain, and each catastrophe serves purposes beyond mortal comprehension. Without Aka's periodic destructions, [[Places/Materia\|Materia]] would choke on its own accumulated growth, unable to renew or transform. The autumn season belongs to Aka. Summer's explosive growth must die back to allow spring's eventual return, and the season demonstrates on a small scale the same principle that volcanic eruptions demonstrate catastrophically. A village buried in ash becomes fertile farmland two generations later. Those who worship Aka do not pray for protection from disasters. They pray for the wisdom to accept destruction as part of nature's cycle, and for the strength to rebuild what must inevitably be swept away.

## Worship and Clergy

### Domains
**Granted Domains:** Destruction, Fire, Strength, Weather

**Subdomains:** Arson, Catastrophe, Ferocity, Seasons

### Holy Symbol
A spiral of red and black, representing destruction spreading outward from its source, like lava flows from volcanic vents or wildfire expanding from initial sparks, consuming all in its path before eventually burning out.

### Sacred Elements
**Sacred Animal:** Scorpion

**Sacred Colors:** Red and Black

### Worshipers

Islanders who live with volcanic activity as constant reality, and those who have survived catastrophes and learned from them, make up Aka's followers. The faith teaches that fighting against natural disasters is futile hubris, that attempting to prevent Aka's destructions only ensures more violent eruptions when pressure finally releases, and that communities must build with the understanding that everything will eventually be swept away.

Temples offer brutal honesty about nature's destructive power and practical training for surviving it. The clergy maintains detailed records of past disasters, kept as proof that destruction always returns and that periods of peace are temporary respites between Aka's necessary clearings. Prediction of the next catastrophe is no part of the purpose. They teach evacuation procedures, disaster preparedness, and the psychological resilience needed to accept that homes, communities, and lives will be lost to forces beyond control. The scorpion carries destruction within itself, kills when threatened, and survives in harsh environments where other creatures perish, its deadly force in the service of survival and not of cruelty.

Priests often live near active volcanoes or in disaster-prone regions where their teachings prove most relevant. They perform controlled burns to prevent more devastating wildfires, on the principle that accepting small destructions prevents catastrophic ones, and they apply that principle well beyond fire. During major disasters, Akan clergy work to maintain order and coordinate evacuations, teaching that panic accomplishes nothing while acceptance allows action. Aka destroys without hatred. Volcanoes do not judge the villages they bury, wildfires do not discriminate between the worthy and unworthy, and tsunamis claim lives with complete indifference to mortal virtue. The clergy counsels against permanent attachment to structures or places, and reminds survivors that cleared ground, though painful to witness, now holds potential for new growth that would have been impossible amid the old.

### Tenets of Faith

Aka's faithful vow to uphold these sacred truths:

1. **Destruction enables creation.** I shall accept that new life requires old growth to be cleared away, whether gently or violently.

2. **Natural disasters serve purposes beyond mortal understanding.** I shall not curse the volcano, the wildfire, or the storm, for they perform necessary work.

3. **All things built will eventually be swept away.** I shall create and construct while accepting that permanence is an illusion.

4. **Attempting to prevent Aka's destructions only ensures more violent eruptions.** I shall accept small catastrophes rather than resist until pressure demands total devastation.

5. **The scorpion kills to survive, not from malice.** I shall understand that destruction is function, not judgment, and that Aka's violence serves balance rather than cruelty.

## The Red Druid Circle

### Overview

The Red never stays in one place. They follow catastrophe across Alora, camping under active volcanoes, on fault lines, in floodplains, on ground still warm from a wildfire. The moment a place calms down and stops threatening to kill them, they pack up. Most circles move every few years. The Red sometimes moves with the season, and a Cinder walker can sleep in a dozen wrecked landscapes between one winter and the next.

They own almost nothing, and they treat attachment itself as a failing. A Red Druid who cannot bear to leave a place, or a person, has already broken faith with Aka's first truth: everything built gets swept away in the end.

### Ways and Practices

Each autumn, they keep the First Clearing in small, burning something they made during the year so the lesson stays in the mind. The roster is never stable. People wander in after living through a flood or an eruption, stay a while, then wander out, and the circle marks neither coming nor going with much ceremony.

### Standing

Outsiders treat them as omens. When the Red Druids walk into a valley, nobody can say whether they came because a disaster is on the way or whether the disaster will arrive because they did.

## Holy Days and Festivals

### The First Clearing

The first hard frost of autumn kills summer's growth in a single night, Aka's destructive power working on the smallest scale, a gentle reminder of the catastrophic force the primordial wields. Followers gather in fields and gardens at dawn when frost's damage becomes fully visible, walking through dying vegetation and offering thanks for the destruction that enables next year's growth.

The ceremony emphasizes that this small destruction clears the way for spring's renewal, the same principle that governs all of Aka's works from frost to volcanic eruption. The clergy leads meditations on accepting loss, of plants and also of the homes, communities, and lives that will inevitably fall to Aka's larger catastrophes. Participants share stories of surviving disasters. The stories dwell on acceptance of nature's power and adaptation to its demands, and no teller claims triumph over it.

Many followers deliberately destroy something they created during the past year, whether a craft project, a written work, or a small structure, physically enacting their acceptance that all creations are temporary and that clinging to them defies nature's design. Controlled burns are performed in areas where fuel has accumulated. The clergy teaches that frost, wildfire, and volcanic eruption differ in scale alone and never in purpose, since all clear away what has become stagnant to enable transformation.

Scorpions are observed where climate permits, their survival in harsh conditions and deadly efficiency held up as examples of how destruction serves life. Some communities build temporary structures specifically to burn during the ceremony, practicing the psychological skill of creating without attachment. The festival concludes with participants planting bulbs that will remain dormant through winter, trusting that Aka's autumn destruction enables spring's eventual renewal. The same principle applies to the catastrophes that destroy cities. New life always follows, given time.
